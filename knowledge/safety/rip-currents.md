# Rip currents
Source: https://www.weather.gov/safety/ripcurrent

A rip current is a narrow, fast channel of water flowing away from the beach, out through the surf zone. Rips form where water pushed ashore by breaking waves finds a path back out to sea — a gap in a sandbar, a channel beside a headland, jetty or pier. They are the leading cause of lifeguard rescues on surf beaches.

Rips can move faster than any swimmer: speeds of 0.5 m/s are common and they can exceed 2 m/s, faster than an Olympic freestyler. They do not pull people under water; they pull people away from shore. The danger is exhaustion from trying to swim straight back against the current.

How to spot one from the sand: a gap in the line of breaking waves where the water looks calmer or darker; a channel of choppy, churning water; foam, seaweed or debris moving steadily out to sea; a plume of discoloured, sandy water beyond the breakers. The "calm" gap between two sets of breaking waves is often the rip, not the safe place.

If you are caught in a rip: do not fight it. Stay calm and float to conserve energy. Swim parallel to the shoreline, across the current, until you are out of it, then swim back to the beach at an angle away from the rip. If you cannot escape, float or tread water and wave and shout for help. Rips are usually narrow and weaken beyond the surf zone.

Prevention: swim near a lifeguard post and between the flags where they exist; check conditions before entering; never swim alone; if in doubt, don't go out. Rips are stronger with bigger surf and around low tide, when more water is squeezed through fewer channels.
