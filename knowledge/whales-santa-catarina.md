# Whales off the Santa Catarina coast
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_right_whale

Two large whales visit the southern Brazilian coast every year, and both can be seen from the beach. The southern right whale (baleia-franca, Eubalaena australis) comes north from sub-Antarctic feeding grounds to calve and nurse in shallow, sheltered bays, typically from June to November with a peak around August and September. Mothers with calves often rest within a few hundred metres of the shore, so calm winter mornings on Santa Catarina's south coast are among the best whale-watching conditions anywhere in the world without a boat.

The stretch of coast from Florianópolis south past Garopaba and Imbituba to Balneário Rincão is a federal environmental protection area created for the right whale, the APA da Baleia Franca. The species was hunted almost to extinction and its recovery here is one of Brazil's conservation successes; it was named "right" by whalers because it was the right whale to hunt, being slow and floating when dead.

Humpback whales (baleia-jubarte, Megaptera novaeangliae) pass the same coast a little further offshore on their migration to breed in the warmer waters of Abrolhos, off Bahia. They are seen off Santa Catarina between about July and November and are the ones that breach — leap clear of the water — and slap the surface with their long white flippers.

Spotting tips: look for the blow, a puff of spray that hangs in the air for a moment; then for a dark back or a raised tail. Right whales have a V-shaped blow and no dorsal fin; humpbacks have a small dorsal fin and show the tail when they dive. Low wind and small waves make the surface easier to read, and morning light from behind you helps. Daylight is the only hard requirement.

Etiquette and rules: Brazilian regulations require boats to keep at least 100 metres from whales and to never approach a mother with a calf head-on. From the beach or a headland there is nothing to worry about — enjoy it. Whales never affect whether the water is safe to swim in; marola shows the sighting likelihood as a nice-to-know only.
