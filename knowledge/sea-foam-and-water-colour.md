# Sea foam, brown water and other things people mistake for pollution (and things they shouldn't)
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_foam

Sea foam is mostly natural. Seawater carries dissolved organic matter — proteins, lipids and the remains of algae — and when waves churn it, that acts like the protein in a cappuccino and whips up a froth. Foam is thickest after algal blooms and after storms, and along beaches with a lot of breaking surf. White or cream foam that comes and goes with the waves is not, by itself, a sign of pollution.

When foam is worth reporting: a persistent brown or grey foam at a stream or drain mouth, foam that smells of sewage or chemicals, or foam with an oily sheen. Those point at an outflow, not at the sea. marola takes pollution sighting reports for exactly this kind of first-hand observation, which reaches people days before a bulletin does.

Brown or muddy water after rain is usually sediment. Rivers and streams carry soil and silt to the sea, and heavy rain turns the water near any outflow the colour of tea for a day or two. Sediment itself is not dangerous, but the same rain that carries it also carries runoff from streets and, where sewage treatment is poor, untreated waste, which is why bathing-water agencies record rainfall at sampling time and why swimming near outflows after a storm is unwise regardless of colour.

Red, rust-coloured or greenish water can be an algal bloom, sometimes called a red tide. Most blooms are harmless, some produce toxins that can irritate skin and eyes and, if shellfish are eaten, cause illness. Local environment agencies issue notices when a bloom is harmful; if the water is discoloured and there is a notice, stay out.

Green water on a clear day is often just plankton — the base of the food web, and after cold-water upwelling it is a sign of a productive sea, not a dirty one. Clear blue water is the sign of very little life in it, which is beautiful and slightly less interesting.
