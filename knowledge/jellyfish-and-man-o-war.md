# Jellyfish, the Portuguese man o' war, and what to do about a sting
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_man_o%27_war

The caravela-portuguesa (Portuguese man o' war, Physalia physalis) is not a jellyfish. It is a siphonophore: a colony of specialised individuals living as one animal beneath a gas-filled float that sits on the surface and sails with the wind. Its tentacles can trail several metres below and behind the float. In southern Brazil it strands on beaches mainly in winter and spring, when persistent onshore winds blow it in from the open ocean, often in numbers.

A man o' war stings on contact with its tentacles, and the sting stays active for hours after the animal is dead on the sand. Never touch one, even dried out, and keep children and dogs away from stranded floats. Stings cause immediate, intense pain and red welts; in rare cases breathing difficulty, chest pain or widespread welts need urgent medical care.

True jellyfish (águas-vivas) are more common in calm, warm water. Warm sea surface temperature, weak wind, small waves and a weak current are the commonly cited conditions for jellyfish accumulating near shore — marola's jellyfish-likelihood heuristic uses exactly those four signals, and it is a heuristic, not a forecast. A visual check of the shoreline before wading in is always worth it when the heuristic says Moderate or High.

First aid for most stings, per widely published guidance: leave the water; rinse the area with seawater, not fresh water (fresh water can trigger more stinging cells); remove any visible tentacle fragments with tweezers or the edge of a card, not bare fingers; immerse the area in hot water, as hot as is comfortable (around 42-45 °C), for 20 to 45 minutes to relieve pain. Guidance on vinegar differs by species and region — it is recommended for box jellyfish but is disputed for the man o' war — so follow local lifeguard guidance rather than a single rule. Seek medical help for stings to the face, for large areas, or for any breathing or heart symptoms.

Reporting: if you see jellyfish or a man o' war stranding, tell the nearest lifeguard post. marola also accepts sighting reports, which are meant to calibrate its heuristic over time.
