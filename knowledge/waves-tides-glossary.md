# Reading the sea: wave height, period, swell, tides and cold-water upwelling
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swell_(ocean)

Wave height in a forecast is the significant wave height: the average of the highest third of waves. Individual waves can be almost twice that, and about one wave in a hundred is roughly one and a half times it. A forecast of 1.0 m therefore means occasional 1.5 to 2 m sets, not a ceiling.

Wave period is the time between successive wave crests, in seconds. It says more about how a wave feels than the height does. A 1 m wave at 5 seconds is short, steep chop that slaps you; the same 1 m at 12 seconds is a long, powerful groundswell that has travelled from a distant storm, breaks with far more energy and drags much more water up and down the beach. For swimming, short period means uncomfortable; long period means strong surges and stronger rips.

Swell and wind waves are reported separately. Swell is the organised, long-period energy from distant weather; wind waves are the local chop raised by today's wind. A forecast with 0.6 m of swell and little wind wave is clean and predictable; the same total height made of wind chop is messy. Swell direction tells you which beaches are exposed: a beach facing the swell takes it full on, a beach around a headland is sheltered.

Tides on the Santa Catarina coast are semi-diurnal, two highs and two lows a day, with a small range of roughly a metre. Sea level is given relative to mean sea level, so a tide of -0.3 m is a low, +0.9 m a high. Low tide exposes sandbars and rocks and concentrates the water returning through fewer channels, so rip currents are typically strongest around low tide; high tide brings deeper water closer to the beach. marola derives the day's tide turns from the hourly sea-level forecast, to the nearest hour.

Upwelling is why summer water in southern Brazil can be surprisingly cold. When north-easterly winds blow persistently along the coast, they push the warm surface layer offshore and cold, nutrient-rich water rises from depth to replace it near the shore; the sea temperature can drop several degrees in a day. It also feeds plankton, which is why the water can look greener afterwards. The classic Brazilian example is Cabo Frio, but the same happens along the Santa Catarina coast.
